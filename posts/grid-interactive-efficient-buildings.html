When utilities control the equipment: grid-interactive efficient buildings in practice

May 2022 · postscript July 2026 · 5 min read

Adapted from an advisory answer I wrote for member utilities at E Source in May 2022, with the question paraphrased. The examples are a snapshot of that moment; a postscript covers what changed by 2026.

We are interested in grid-interactive efficient buildings (GEBs). Can you share examples of utility pilots that directly control equipment inside buildings, such as HVAC, water heating, EV charging, or storage? What are the main challenges?

In 2022, the benefits of grid-interactive buildings were still being proven through pilots. The US Department of Energy’s national GEB roadmap (May 2021) laid out how energy efficiency and demand flexibility could be combined, and its Building Technologies Office defined the goal:

“The GEB vision is the integration and continuous optimization of DERs for the benefit of building owners and occupants, as well as the grid.”

Programme design split by building size. Homes and small commercial buildings got incentives for individual smart devices. Large buildings needed integrated energy management information systems (EMIS) with sensors and controls; NYSERDA’s Real-Time Energy Management programme, for example, paid incentives for qualified EMIS purchases.

A few utilities combined efficiency and demand response in one programme. NV Energy’s Business Solutions Center helped commercial customers install efficiency measures and smart thermostats, then auto-enrolled qualifying devices into demand response. That was the exception: as ACEEE documented, most utility programmes kept efficiency and flexibility in separate silos.

What direct control looked like

Software-based control of DERs

In April 2022, National Grid New York piloted Leap’s distributed energy resource platform at four New York City office buildings. Edo worked with Avista on building-to-grid demonstrations across the US.

Subscription bundles

Uplight’s Plus packaged multiple utility programmes around customer needs, from efficiency enrolment to thermostat optimisation via demand response, piloted with AES and Duke Energy.

Automated demand response

PG&E’s WatterSaver pilot with Virtual Peaker aimed to put Wi-Fi-enabled heat pump water heaters in five to nine thousand homes, heating water when grid power was clean and storing it for when it wasn’t. Duke Energy Carolina’s EnergyWise Business provided free smart thermostats and HVAC switches. In the Pacific Northwest, BPA, Portland General Electric, and NEEA quantified the peak-shifting value of grid-connected water heaters.

Behavioural outreach

National Grid sent personalised energy reports to customers in Massachusetts and New York, matching demand-response offers to the equipment in each home.

Building management systems as a service

CPower, working with NYISO, offered tenant-by-tenant, floor-by-floor control with analytics, pitched partly at Local Law 97 compliance.

What held programmes back

Three things. Valuation: no fair, transparent way to price stacked efficiency and grid benefits, so owners could not see why participation was worth it. Silos: efficiency and flexibility lived in separate programmes with separate goals and cost tests. Maturity: whole-building automation responding to grid signals was still emerging, under evaluation in programmes like GSA’s Proving Ground.

The way forward, from 2022

Four requirements: scale aggregation at the device, vendor, and building level; redesign rates so the value of flexibility is clear and lasts as long as the equipment; build valuation frameworks, where PNNL was developing cost-effectiveness approaches; and stand up collaboration test beds to validate performance, as LBNL argued.

Postscript · July 2026

The clearest change is the banner. What the roadmap called GEBs has consolidated into the virtual power plant framing. Wood Mackenzie puts US VPP capacity at 37.5 GW, growing about 14% a year, and DOE’s Liftoff analysis argues that tripling it to 80–160 GW by 2030 could cover 10–20% of peak demand and save about $10 billion a year.

The motivation shifted from decarbonisation to capacity adequacy. With data centres, manufacturing, and electrification pushing US peak demand from roughly 800 GW toward 900 GW by 2030, flexible buildings became one of the few resources that can arrive in time.

The market plumbing aged worst. FERC Order 2222, which opens wholesale markets to aggregated building-scale resources, is still uneven: CAISO finished compliance in late 2024; PJM has slipped toward 2027–28. And the barrier in the original answer, fair valuation of stacked benefits, is still the binding constraint. The technology matured faster than the incentives. True in 2022, still true now.
