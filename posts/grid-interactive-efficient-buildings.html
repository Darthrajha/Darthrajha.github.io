When utilities control the equipment: grid-interactive efficient buildings in practice

May 2022 · postscript July 2026 · 8 min read

Adapted from an advisory answer I wrote for member utilities during my time at E Source (May 2022), lightly edited for the web with the member question paraphrased. The original programme examples are preserved as a snapshot of that moment; a postscript at the end traces where the field went next.

We are interested in grid-interactive efficient buildings (GEBs). Can you share examples of utility pilots or programmes where the utility directly controls equipment inside buildings — thermal storage, HVAC controls, connected lighting, EV charging, solar PV, battery storage? What are the main challenges, and how would you overcome them?

In 2022, the benefits of grid-interactive efficient buildings were still being established through small-scale implementations and pilots. The US Department of Energy’s national GEB roadmap, released in May 2021, set out recommendations for building-sector stakeholders to unlock savings by combining energy efficiency with demand flexibility. DOE’s Building Technologies Office summarised the ambition well:

“The GEB vision is the integration and continuous optimization of DERs for the benefit of building owners and occupants, as well as the grid.”

The programme design split by building size. For homes and small commercial buildings, smart energy management programmes promoted and incentivised individual smart devices. Large commercial buildings needed more complex offerings — integrated energy management information systems (EMIS) with advanced sensors and controls. NYSERDA’s Real-Time Energy Management (RTEM) programme, for instance, paid incentives to large commercial and multifamily customers purchasing EMIS from qualified vendors.

A few utilities went further and offered a single streamlined programme spanning both efficiency and demand response: NV Energy’s Business Solutions Center helped commercial customers identify and install efficiency measures and smart thermostats, then auto-enrolled qualifying devices into demand-response programmes. But that was the exception. As the American Council for an Energy-Efficient Economy (ACEEE) documented at the time, most utility programmes remained segregated, with separate goals for efficiency and for grid flexibility.

What direct control looked like on the ground

Software-based control of DERs

In April 2022, National Grid New York announced a pilot with Leap, a distributed energy resource software platform, at four New York City office buildings — aiming, per the press release, to “reduce New York’s dependency on the least efficient and most expensive electrical generation options, increase grid stability, and bring down the cost of electricity for all.” Edo, a startup focused on building-to-grid integration, was working with Avista on several demonstration projects across the US.

Subscription bundles

Mirroring the Netflix model, Uplight launched Plus — packaging multiple utility programmes around customer needs, from efficiency programme enrolment to smart-thermostat optimisation via demand response — piloted with AES, Duke Energy, and others.

Automated demand response

Pacific Gas & Electric’s WatterSaver pilot with Virtual Peaker set out to install or retrofit Wi-Fi-enabled heat pump water heaters across five to nine thousand customers — heating water when grid power was clean and plentiful, storing it for when it wasn’t. Duke Energy Carolina’s EnergyWise Business programme provided free smart thermostats and Wi-Fi switches for HVAC equipment, installation included. In the Pacific Northwest, a collaborative pilot by Bonneville Power Administration, Portland General Electric, and NEEA quantified the peak-reduction and load-shifting value of grid-connected water heaters.

Behavioural outreach

National Grid sent personalised energy-use reports to customers in Massachusetts and New York, with device-specific insights and demand-response promotion tailored to the equipment and usage profiles in each home.

Building management systems as a service

CPower, working with NYISO, launched building-management-system-as-a-service: advanced analytics with tenant-by-tenant, floor-by-floor control — pitched partly at New York’s Local Law 97 compliance market.

What held these programmes back

The biggest barrier was valuation: determining the fair, transparent value of combined efficiency and grid benefits, and making sure building owners and occupants could see why participation was worth it. Programme silos compounded this — efficiency and demand flexibility lived in separate programmes with separate goals and separate cost-effectiveness tests. And technological maturity was a real constraint: coordinated whole-building automation responding to grid signals was still an emerging capability, then under evaluation in demonstration programmes like GSA’s Proving Ground.

The way forward, as it looked from 2022

Four things needed to happen. Aggregation had to scale — at the device, vendor, and building level — to bring down the cost of grid-responsive sophistication. Rate structures had to be redesigned so the financial value of flexibility was clear and could be locked in over equipment investment lifecycles. Valuation frameworks had to catch up, with approaches for demonstrating cost-effectiveness of GEB measures emerging from Pacific Northwest National Laboratory. And collaboration test beds — individual buildings, campuses, commercial developments — were needed to validate demand-flexibility performance and demonstrate value to both the utility system and building owners, as Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory argued.

Postscript — July 2026

Four years on, the clearest change is the banner the work happens under: what the 2021 roadmap called grid-interactive efficient buildings has largely consolidated into the virtual power plant framing. The behind-the-meter flexible capacity that pilots like these seeded has become an asset class — Wood Mackenzie puts US VPP capacity at 37.5 GW, growing about 14% a year, and DOE’s Pathways to Commercial Liftoff analysis argues that tripling that to 80–160 GW by 2030 could cover 10–20% of peak demand and save roughly $10 billion a year in grid costs.

The motivation shifted too. In 2022 the case for grid-interactive buildings was framed mostly around decarbonisation and avoided peaker dispatch. Today it is framed around capacity adequacy: with data centres, re-shored manufacturing, and electrified heating and transport pushing US peak demand from roughly 800 GW towards 900 GW by 2030, flexible buildings stopped being a nice-to-have and became one of the few resources that can show up in time.

What has aged least well is the market plumbing. FERC Order 2222 — the rule meant to open wholesale markets to aggregated building-scale resources — is still being implemented unevenly: CAISO completed compliance in late 2024, while PJM’s timeline has slipped toward 2027–28. And the barrier I called out in the original answer — fair, transparent valuation of stacked efficiency and flexibility benefits — remains the binding constraint on scale. The technology matured faster than the incentives. That was true in 2022; it is still true now.
